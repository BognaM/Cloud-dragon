AI and mental health: what the research says

A short, plain-language summary of 22 studies and expert reports (2017–2026) on using AI chatbots for emotional support and self-help. It's a summary, not a formal meta-analysis: the numbers come from the individual studies listed at the end. Last updated October 2026.

In short

  • Structured exercises can help, for a while. Chatbots built to guide proven techniques, such as exercises from cognitive behavioural therapy, reduced depression and distress in trials, mostly over 2–8 weeks.
  • People value them because they're always available and don't judge.
  • The same problems keep appearing: agreeing too easily, weak handling of crises, stigma, simulated empathy, and dependence that grows with heavy use.
  • AI works best alongside people, not instead of them.

Many people already use AI for support

In a 2025 US survey of 12–21-year-olds, 13% had asked AI for advice when they felt sad, angry or nervous (22% of 18–21-year-olds), and 93% of them found it helpful. In interviews, people describe AI as a non-judgmental "emotional sanctuary" they can reach at any time, often to fill gaps between other kinds of care.

What helps

  • Purpose-built therapy chatbots. In the first randomized trial of a generative therapy chatbot (Therabot, 2025), adults with depression saw their symptoms drop by about half over 4 weeks, compared with people on a waiting list. Participants felt a bond with it similar to one with a human therapist. Clinicians monitored the trial, and its authors stress the bot isn't safe without that oversight.
  • Across many studies, chatbots had moderate effects on depression and distress, but no clear effect on overall well-being (a meta-analysis of 15 trials).
  • Guided techniques, at scale. When an AI walked 15,531 people through rethinking a negative thought, 67% felt their emotions become less intense. Teenagers benefited less, until the AI used simpler language.

What can go wrong

  • Agreeing too easily. Chatbots learn to tell people what they want to hear ("sycophancy"). In a mental health setting, that can mean validating distorted or harmful thoughts.
  • Crises and stigma. Tested against clinical guidelines, popular models responded inappropriately to suicidal thoughts and delusions, and showed stigma toward conditions like alcohol dependence and schizophrenia. Newer, larger models were no safer.
  • Simulated empathy. Psychologists and peer counselors who reviewed AI counseling sessions found 15 ethical risks, including "deceptive empathy": caring words that create a false sense of connection.
  • Dependence. In a 4-week study of 981 people, heavier daily chatbot use went with more loneliness, more emotional dependence and less time with other people.
  • Echo chambers. A chatbot that adapts to you and agrees with you can reinforce your beliefs in a loop, which may strengthen delusions in vulnerable people.

How sure can we be?

Not very, yet. Trials are short, usually compare the chatbot with a waiting list rather than with real therapy, and are often run by the people who built the tool. Interview studies involve small groups of around 20 people. Popular general chatbots, where most real use happens, have mostly been studied through surveys and lab tests, not long-term outcomes. In November 2025, the American Psychological Association warned that most of these tools weren't built for clinical use and lack validation, safety protocols and regulatory approval.

Using an AI companion wisely

  • Use it for something specific: putting feelings into words, a short exercise, planning your day.
  • Be wary when it always agrees. Ask it to question you, and check important things with a person.
  • Notice how it fits into your life. If you spend more time with a chatbot and less with people, take a step back.
  • It can't help in a crisis. In Switzerland, call 143 to talk to someone, or 144 in an emergency.
